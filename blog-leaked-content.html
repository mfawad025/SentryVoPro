What to Do If Your Content Gets Leaked

Finding your own content posted somewhere without permission is unsettling. Here's a clear order of operations, not a panic response.

1. Document before you do anything else

Before requesting removal, capture proof it existed: a screenshot with the URL and date visible, and if possible, save or archive the page itself. Once content is taken down, you lose your easiest evidence that it was ever there — which matters if the same leak reappears later or you need to show a pattern of repeat infringement.

2. Identify every place it appears — not just the first one

Leaked content rarely stays in one place. A single leak often gets re-posted across forums, file-sharing sites, and social platforms within days. Search variations of your username, stage name, and any distinctive details before filing anything, so you're not repeating this process every time a new copy surfaces.

3. File with the platform first, the host second

Major platforms usually have a faster, purpose-built copyright report form — start there. If the leak is on an independent site with no responsive owner, escalate to the actual hosting provider (found via a WHOIS/RDAP lookup on the domain), since they can act even when the site owner won't.

4. Don't negotiate directly with whoever posted it

It's tempting to message the person who leaked it directly, but this rarely helps and can complicate a later legal notice — it can be read as tacit permission-seeking, or simply escalate into harassment in the other direction. Route everything through formal reporting channels instead.

5. Watch for reposts after removal

A takedown on one copy doesn't prevent new copies appearing elsewhere. This is the part that becomes genuinely exhausting to do manually — which is the actual problem an ongoing scanning service like SentryVo is built to solve: catching new instances automatically instead of you having to re-search everything by hand.

6. If it involves your personal information too

If the leak includes identifying details beyond the content itself — real name, address, or other personal information tied to your accounts — that's a separate, more serious situation. Depending on where you are, this may warrant contacting local authorities in addition to filing takedowns, particularly if it's paired with threats or harassment.
