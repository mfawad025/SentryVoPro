How to Remove Leaked Content From Google Search

Getting a leaked page out of Google search will not delete it from the website, but it stops people finding it. Here is how the request works.

What removing a page from Google does

When you send Google a valid copyright removal request, Google can stop showing that page in its search results. The page itself stays online at the website that hosts it, and anyone with the direct link can still open it. That is why creators usually do both: they ask Google to remove the page from search and they send a notice to the website or its host.

Removing a page from search is still worth doing. Most people who stumble on a leak find it through a search for the creator's name, so taking those pages out of the results cuts most of the traffic they get.

Before you start

  • Only report content you own. A request must be made by the copyright owner or someone authorized to act for them. A false request can have legal consequences for the person who files it.
  • Collect the exact addresses. Google needs the full link to each page that copies your work, such as example.com/your-name/photo-123, not just the site's home page.
  • Have the original ready. You will be asked to say what the original work is and where it lives, for example your official page or profile.
  • Check each page first. Open the link and confirm it really shows your content. Pages that only mention your name are not copyright infringement.

How to submit the request

  1. Open Google's copyright removal form (search for "Google DMCA removal request" or go to reportcontent.google.com/forms/dmca_search).
  2. Choose Google Search as the product.
  3. Describe your original work and give the address where the original appears.
  4. Paste the addresses of the pages you want removed from search results. You can list several in one request.
  5. Add the Google search result address if the form asks for it, then read and confirm the required statements.
  6. Enter your contact details and your signature, then submit. Keep the confirmation email.

What happens next

Google reviews the request. If it is valid, it removes the pages from results and tells you. Google may also pass a copy of the notice, with some details left out, to the Lumen database, a public research archive of takedown requests. The website owner can also be told and can send a counter-notice if they believe the removal was a mistake. Plan for that: do not put anything in the request that you would not want a website owner to read.

Response times vary. Some requests are handled within days, others take longer. If a request is rejected, the email usually says why, for example a missing address or content that Google could not match to your original.

Mistakes that get requests rejected

  • Listing a site's home page or a search page instead of the page with your content.
  • Not saying where your original work is, so Google cannot compare the two.
  • Reporting a page that does not actually copy your work, such as one that only links to it or mentions your name.
  • Typing links by hand and getting them wrong. Copy and paste each one.

Keeping it up over time

New copies appear all the time, so one request is not the end of it. Search for your name every week, note every new page, and repeat the process. Keep a simple record of what you reported and on what date, so you can see what was removed and what came back.
