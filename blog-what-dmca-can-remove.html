DMCA Takedowns for Creators: What You Can and Cannot Remove

A DMCA notice is a copyright tool. It works well for stolen copies of your work and not at all for some problems that feel similar.

What a copyright notice is for

The DMCA lets a copyright owner ask a website, host or search engine to remove a copy of their original work that was posted without permission. It is about copies of your creative work: photos, videos, audio, writing and artwork that you made.

What you can usually remove

  • Your photos and videos re-uploaded without permission, on tube sites, forums, file hosts, social platforms or Telegram.
  • Clips cut from your longer videos, if they are taken from your work.
  • Your content copied onto a fake account that pretends to be you, as long as the content is your own work.
  • Search results that point to those copies. You can ask Google to stop showing the pages in search.

What a copyright notice cannot remove

  • Your name on its own. A page that only mentions you, or a thread of people talking about you, does not copy your work.
  • Content you do not own. If another photographer took the picture, they may own the copyright unless your agreement says otherwise. Check before you file.
  • Commentary, news and criticism. Some uses are allowed without permission, such as short quotations used for review. These are judged case by case, and a platform may refuse a notice or restore the content.
  • Pages that only link to your content. Whether a link can be reported depends on the platform. Report the page that holds the copy where you can.

Problems that need a different report

  • A fake account that uses your name or face but does not copy your works: use the platform's impersonation report.
  • Private or intimate images shared without your consent: most large platforms and Google have separate reporting routes for this, and the free service StopNCII.org can help stop images being shared on participating platforms. These do not require you to own the copyright.
  • Harassment or threats: use the platform's safety report, and contact the police if you feel unsafe.

Before you file: three checks

  1. Do you own it? You made it, or the rights were assigned to you in writing.
  2. Is it really your content? Open the page and compare it with your original.
  3. Did you give permission? If you or a partner posted it, licensed it or shared it with a buyer who was allowed to share it, a notice will be wrong.

Sending a notice for content you do not own or have no right to remove can lead to legal trouble for the person who sends it. If you are unsure, ask a lawyer who handles copyright before you send it. This guide is general information, not legal advice, and rules differ between countries.

What to expect

Large platforms and search engines usually have a clear process and act on valid notices. Smaller or overseas sites can be slow or ignore notices, and then the usual next step is a notice to the company that hosts the site. Content can also come back under a new link, so tracking what you reported matters as much as the notice itself.
